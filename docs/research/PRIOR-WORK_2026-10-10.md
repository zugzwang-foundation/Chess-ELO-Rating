# Prior work and what this proposal adds: Ghita, Sonas, Glickman, URS, the Kaggle contests, WHR

Status: REVIEW — a record for the v0.4 proposal (ELO-4 brief, Phase 5.1–5.2), folded into v0.4 in Phase 6; never edited after the fact. Author: The Zugzwang Authors · Licence: CC BY 4.0 (`docs/LICENSE-docs.md`) · Date: 2026-10-10.

**Sources and rules.** Vlad Ghita's book, *The Rating Revolution* (v1.0, February 2026) [R 5], the operator's purchased copy, is cited by printed page and paraphrased; none of its tables is reproduced and no quotation runs beyond a short phrase. Every other claim about another work rests on the primary-source sweep `docs/research/VERIFICATION_PRIOR-WORK.md` ([VP 1] to [VP 12]) or on the research report [R n]; where a claim could not be checked, it says so. Our own evidence is cited as [E n]. "Ours" means the design of the v0.3 proposal and annex as revised by the architect's rulings R1–R14 (`docs/decisions/D-0008_architect-rulings-elo-4.md`); rung numbers are the adoption ladder's (proposal §9).

**A naming clash, first.** Ghita's repair has two "layers": his Layer 1 recalibrates the expectancy curve for everyone, his Layer 2 clears federation-level debt ([R 5], p. 49). Our layers are different in kind: Layer 0 replicates FIDE's rules, Layer 1 is a statistical model that never edits a rating, Layer 2 is the published rating. His Layer 1 corresponds to our rung 2, his Layer 2 to our rung 7 and to parts of rungs 3, 5 and 6.

## 1 Ghita's repair, mechanism by mechanism (Phase 5.1)

His repair is Part V of the book (pp. 48–60), with its policy context in Appendix B (pp. 69–73). He states the constraints first: keep Elo's interpretability and continuity, keep title thresholds meaningful, and make every intervention testable, reversible and auditable; Glicko is set aside for its complexity and the break in continuity, in favour of a minimal-Elo repair (p. 48).

**Where ours matches his and he proposed it first.** The federation diagnostic and its weighted, recursive method (G3); treating the expectancy scale as a monitored, capped quantity (G1, with Sonas before both on the curve itself); an activity-linked bonus as the injection channel (G5); explicit safeguards with a sunset (G7, partly); the deflation-tax framing (G10). The proposal says so in its text.

**Where ours differs, and why.** No resets (G6), for the reasons in that row and the deflation evidence of E5; no federation action before FIDE's game data pass a selection test (G3, G5), because the only game data we can test on are biased toward strong players [E7]; three outcomes and colour in the table rather than one scale (G1), because a single scale cannot fit both the level-dependent draws and the colour term [E2]; compensation in the expectation rather than bonuses to the junior (G10), so that ratings move only through results.

## 2 Prior work and what this proposal adds (Phase 5.2)

### 2.1 Ghita: book and posts

- **Posts.** His June 2025 essay (Substack, republished by the Belgian federation) found clustering near 1500 with a pile-up at the 1400 floor, an average fall of about one point a month despite about 3,500 new players a month, and federation gaps against URS ratings; he suggested that FIDE track and display URS ratings for a year and later run a Glicko-2 type system in parallel [VP 2]. His February 2026 post summarised the book's federation analysis: about 200,000 cross-border classical games of 2025, a per-game weight E(1 − E) with the odds capped at 90/10, and a recursion that converged in five iterations to below 0.1 point; it proposes no repair and defers to the book [VP 1].
- **Where our evidence agrees.** Favourites are over-predicted by the 400-point curve (his S = 459; our fitted table and E2's residuals [E2]); federation residuals point the way he says [E7]; juniors improve far faster than K = 40 lets their ratings follow (his Part IV; Layer 1's age drift is +193 points a year under 12 and +79 at 16–19, `analysis/OUTPUT_L1_history.md`).
- **Where it differs.** His figures of 26 and 16 points a year lost by the median active player before and after March 2024 (p. 21) are the change of the cross-sectional median of the active list; on FIDE's lists that median falls only because entrants arrive below the players who stop, while continuing players gain, and the level of steadily active adults has not fallen since March 2024 [E5]. The pile-up at the 1400 floor that his 2025 essay reports does not appear on the published lists, where fewer players sit at 1400 than at each of the next nine points; players who fall below the floor leave the list, so the lists cannot show a pile-up beneath it [E1]. His Deflation Index and scale are on FIDE's non-public extract (pp. 63–65), so our equivalents use other games [E5].

### 2.2 Sonas: the 2023 proposal FIDE hosted, its supplemental and final reports

- **What he proposed.** A one-time compression of every rating below 2000 by 0.40 × (2000 − R), mapping 1000–2000 onto 1400–2000, and permanent "calculation improvements": a 1400 floor, initial ratings with two hypothetical draws against 1800, plus scores rated as performance ratings, the 400-point rule restored, the same changes in rapid and blitz [VP 3, 4, 5]. FIDE's regulations from 1 March 2024 carry the floor, the hypothetical draws and the 400-point rule [V 1], and the lists show the compression applied as he specified [E5]. He proposed no change to K and, having looked at changing the expectancy table, judged that it would not help [VP 3, 5]. His diagnosis: ratings too spread out, with the initial-rating formula the main engine [VP 3, 5]. His evidence: empirical score curves over 13 million games, shallower than the table and growing shallower; a replica of 15 years of FIDE calculations used to simulate the changes [VP 3, 4].
- **Where our evidence agrees.** The curve is shallower than table 8.1.2 [E2]; the published scale is more spread out than strength, and the fitted table carries that spread (R9) [E2]; and on FIDE's lists the compression did what he meant it to do for the level: the steady-adult cohort stopped losing points from March 2024 [E5], which credits his intervention.
- **Where it differs.** He changed ratings once and the newcomer rule permanently, and left the expectancy table alone; our evidence on broadcast games is that a fitted three-outcome table passes the calibration rules out of sample where table 8.1.2 fails [E2], a result on forecast calibration rather than on system simulation, so the two judgements are not about the same test. He foresaw that further steps might be needed and named the weak point: K = 40 juniors exchanging points mostly with each other, so that nothing enters the pool; he pointed to Chess Scotland's junior additions as a possible next step and expected the QC to keep monitoring [VP 5]. Our rung 5 is that kind of step, with the difference set out in 2.6; the monitoring he expected is our rung 6 and the monthly report.
- **The supplemental report and URS.** The research report says the supplemental report weighed URS as an auxiliary rating [R §4]. The report quotes that suggestion from a community comment by Ghita and does not evaluate it [VP 4]; the research report's summary is corrected here.

### 2.3 Glickman: Glicko, Glicko-2 and the US Chess monitoring

- **Glicko** gives every rating a deviation RD; the update's multiplier, q·RD′², plays the role of K and is larger for an uncertain player; RD grows with time away by a constant c and shrinks with games [VP 6]. Rung 4's K_i is the same idea, which Glickman published first: the gain of one game given the model's uncertainty about the player. Differences: our σ_i comes from Layer 1 (a whole-history model over three time controls with age-dependent drift), K_i is printed and enters an Elo-style update on the published scale with the 1/κ factor of R6, and the opponent's uncertainty does not damp the game term as Glicko's g(RD_j) does.
- **Glicko-2** adds a per-player volatility σ that sets how fast RD grows, constrained by a system constant τ [VP 7]. Layer 1 instead fixes the rate of change by age band (the annex's σ_θ profile, scaled by a fitted c_θ), so a junior's estimate is allowed to move faster than an adult's by rule, not by detected volatility.
- **US Chess.** Glickman's ratings committee tracks the mean rating of "stable" players, active for four years and aged 35–45, with the goal of holding the 1997 level, finds that ratings continue to deflate, and lowered the bonus threshold to 12 [VP 12]. Our anchor cohort (aged 25–45, steadily active) and the rung 6 controller do the same job in published form, a credit the proposal already gives [R 22].

### 2.4 URS: one rating across time controls

URS (Sonas, Glickman, Miller and Rischard; first lists 2016–2017; funded by the Grand Chess Tour and partners) publishes one rating for classical strength informed by every over-the-board game down to five minutes, with faster games weighted less, a rapid and a blitz "gap" per player, older games discounted exponentially over 72 months, all players re-estimated together, and a prior from the typical strength of players of the same age for new players; ratings can change without games and histories were recalculated when the method changed [VP 8]. Layer 1 shares that structure: one shared strength, per-time-control offsets, age-based priors, all players fitted together. URS came first: credit. The difference is where the model sits: URS is itself the published rating; ours keeps three forward-only lists that change only when a player plays, and lets the model in only through named, capped channels.

### 2.5 The Kaggle contests (2010, 2011)

- **2010, "Elo versus the Rest of the World".** About 73,000 games between some 8,000 players; won by Sismanis's Elo++, one logistic rating per player with a White advantage (about 35 points), time-weighted games and regularisation toward the average of each player's opponents, with two global parameters [VP 9]; plain Elo finished 141st of 258 [VP 10]. Rung 2's colour term (36 points in standard [E2]) is the same White term, which Elo++ already used.
- **2011, the Deloitte/FIDE challenge.** About two million games among 54,000 players from FIDE-rated events, 1999–2009; won by Salimans with a Bayesian ordered-probit model whose edge came partly from the match schedule, which he called less than ideal for a rating system; plain Elo finished 80th of 189 and the leaders were judged about 15 % more accurate [VP 10]. Our protocol never uses the schedule [R 9]. FIDE also ran a prize for the most practical system; its outcome could not be verified from a primary source [VP 10]. Ghita names Alec Stephenson's system, Glicko with a White term, an activity bonus and an opponent-strength term, and reports that FIDE found it too hard to understand (pp. 71–72); not verified here.
- **What the contests show.** Systems more accurate than Elo have existed since 2010; what FIDE has adopted since are the simple, explainable pieces [R §4]. That is why our model stays behind the published rating.

### 2.6 Chess Scotland's junior additions

According to IM Douglas Bryson's comment in FIDE's 2023 consultation, which Sonas quoted [VP 4], Chess Scotland has since 1976 added an age-dependent number of points to a junior's grade when computing the opponent's expected score, also in games between two juniors, so that points enter the pool even when juniors play each other; Sonas later named it a possible next step for FIDE [VP 5]. Rung 5 is the same mechanism, and we credit it: the opponent's expectation uses a higher number for the junior while the junior's own rating is computed as usual. Ours differs twice. The addition is c_j, the part of the gap Layer 1 is 90 % sure of less a margin, from the junior's same-time-control evidence (R5), rather than a schedule by age. And it applies only against opponents who are not themselves eligible (R8), because compensating both sides breaks the rule that a junior's own update uses published ratings and creates 10 to 26 points a game (REDTEAM_v0_3, V3-STAT-8); the junior–junior exchange Sonas named as the weak point is therefore left to rung 6. On broadcast games the mechanism cuts the adults' drain against eligible juniors by two-thirds, from −0.055 to −0.018 a game, and improves forecasts, but leaves the drain from juniors whose compensation is zero, so the pre-registered ±0.01 rule is not met [E6]; Chess Scotland's age schedule would reach those juniors, at the cost of compensating some who are not under-rated.

### 2.7 Whole-History Rating

Coulom's WHR computes the exact maximum a posteriori of every player's whole rating history under a dynamic Bradley–Terry model with a Wiener-process prior, by Newton steps per player on a tridiagonal Hessian, with the covariance from the inverse Hessian, and revises past estimates as evidence arrives [VP 11]. Layer 1's fit is WHR's method, credited since v0.1 [R 11]: ours adds three outcomes, colour, level-dependent draws, time-control offsets, age-dependent drift and an anchor, and is fitted on a rolling window. The retroactive revision WHR accepts is why the model sits behind a forward-only published rating.
